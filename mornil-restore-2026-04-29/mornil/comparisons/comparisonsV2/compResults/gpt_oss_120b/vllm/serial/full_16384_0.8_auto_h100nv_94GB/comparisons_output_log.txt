## 3.  Scientific / evolutionary view  

* **Life as a by‑product of chemistry** – From a strictly biological standpoint, the “purpose” of living organisms is to survive, reproduce, and pass on genes (the classic *selfish gene* idea).  
* **Consciousness adds a layer** – Once a species develops self‑awareness, it can reflect on its own existence and assign symbolic or cultural meanings that go far beyond mere replication.  
* **Meaning as an emergent property** – Many scientists and philosophers (e.g., Daniel Dennett, Sam Harris) argue that meaning is a mental construct that helps us navigate a complex world; it’s not “out there” but still real in the sense that it shapes behavior and wellbeing.

**Takeaway:** Science can explain *how* life works, but it remains neutral on *why* we should feel that our lives matter—those “why” questions are answered by the mind, culture, or personal narrative.

## 4.  Psychological and sociological insights  

1. **Purpose → Well‑being** – Studies (e.g., Viktor Frankl’s logotherapy, modern positive‑psychology research) show that people who perceive a purpose report higher life satisfaction, resilience, and even better physical health.  
2. **Three common sources of meaning** (according to research by psychologists such as Roy Baumeister & Emily C. Smith):  
   - **Belonging** – Relationships, community, love.  
   - **Coherence** – Understanding the world, making sense of events.  
   - **Transcendence** – Feeling part of something larger (nature, art, a cause).  
3. **Narrative identity** – We construct a life story that links past, present, and future; the more coherent and positively oriented that story, the stronger the sense of meaning.

3. **The “Future‑Self” scenario** – Picture yourself 80 years from now, looking back. Which actions would you be proud of? Which regrets would you wish you’d avoided? This can highlight what you already consider meaningful.

### Why the blue whale is the biggest

- **Evolutionary niche:** As filter‑feeders, blue whales can exploit the abundant, tiny krill that swarms in massive numbers in polar and sub‑polar waters. Their enormous size lets them store huge energy reserves for long migrations between feeding and breeding grounds.
- **Physiology:** Their massive, streamlined bodies reduce drag, and a thick layer of blubber provides insulation and buoyancy. Their respiratory system is adapted for huge lung capacity (up to ~5,000 L of air) and rapid surface breathing.

- A blue whale’s heart is about the size of a small car and beats only 8–10 times per minute when the animal is at the surface.

- If a blue whale were placed in a swimming pool, the water would rise by roughly 2 m (6 ft) due to its displacement.

### Conservation status

- **IUCN Red List:** Endangered. Populations were decimated by commercial whaling in the 20th century (down to ~1 % of pre‑whaling numbers).
